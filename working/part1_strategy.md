## §2. Guiding principle

**Activate, don't notify.**

Every post-purchase touchpoint exists to advance the user toward redemption — not to inform them about what they bought. Comms that don't move the user one concrete step closer to use are not "softer engagement"; they are noise we are accountable for sending. The product's job after checkout is to do as much of the activation work *for* the user as possible (pick the date, set the reminder, surface the merchant phone with one tap) so that "having bought" and "having used" feel like the same continuous action, not two separate decisions.

This principle survives across deal types and platforms because it's about *whose work it is*. Local needs us to book the slot; goods needs us to surface the moment of arrival; gifting needs us to do the activation work for the giftee specifically. The medium changes; the principle doesn't.

**What I am willing to deprioritize: short-term refund volume.**

A commitment mechanic surfaces redemption intent earlier in the user's journey. Surfacing intent earlier *will* surface merchant-side friction (overbooking, no-shows, refused honor) that today is hidden by the simple fact that the user never tried. Some of that friction will convert to refund requests instead of silent churn. I am willing to absorb a measurable short-term lift in refund volume in exchange for: (a) visible signal about which merchants are creating the friction, (b) the long-term repeat-rate gain from the users we successfully activate, and (c) the elimination of silent churn, which is worse than visible refunds because it bleeds the metric we actually care about. Refund cost is a real ledger line. I'll defend it as a deliberate cost, not optimize it away.

## §3. Redemption → Retention as a system

Three layers, in order. Each layer must function for the next to make sense.

**Layer 1 — Activation (first 48 hours).**
The post-checkout experience asks "when will you use this?" in a deal-type-aware way. For a restaurant, deep-link to the booking partner with the deal pre-applied. For health & wellness, surface the merchant phone with one tap and a "call now / remind me later today" choice, plus a calendar slot picker. For events, the date is already specified — confirm calendar add and set a 24-hour-before nudge. For walk-in services, surface merchant location, hours, and a "use within X" target the user picks themselves. The output of Layer 1 is a *commitment artifact* — a calendar entry, a confirmed booking, a scheduled reminder — owned by the user, generated by us.

**Layer 2 — Hold (between commit and redemption).**
A single, well-timed nudge tied to the user's commitment, not to a Groupon cadence. If they booked a slot, the nudge is 24 hours before. If they set "use within two weeks," the nudge is 48 hours before the deadline they chose. If they did neither (Layer 1 fell through), one fallback nudge fires at day 7 with a "let's get this on the calendar" CTA — explicitly framed as a re-entry into Layer 1, not as a "don't forget" reminder. The Hold layer is sparse on purpose. The discipline is: nudges fire against user-set context, never blast cadence. This is how we reduce TTR without being pushy.

**Layer 3 — Repeat (post-redemption only).**
"Buy Again" / next-purchase prompts surface **only after a confirmed redemption with a positive merchant rating**, **within the recall window** for that deal type (within 7 days for local services, immediately after delivery for goods, within 24 hours after the event for events), and **in the deal-type-relevant category** (a massage redeemer sees other wellness deals, not garage doors).

**Buy Again must not surface:** before redemption (dilutes the redemption commitment); after a refund or no-show (insulting and trust-eroding); inside mid-task flows on another deal (cart, checkout); to giftees who haven't yet redeemed (turns gifting into acquisition and damages trust on both sides). The principle: Buy Again is earned by a positive experience, not triggered by a successful transaction.

**How this reduces TTR for local without pressure:** the mechanism is *not more reminders*. It's moving the commitment decision into the moment of highest intent (the second after they pay). Asking "when will you use this?" at checkout is service, not nag. Layer 2's nudges then run against the user's own commitment, so they read as "the thing you said you wanted, on time" rather than "Groupon poking you again."

## §4. Experiments — two for the first 60 days

### E1. "Plan-it" — commitment mechanic at post-checkout (local services)

**Hypothesis.** Local-services first-purchase buyers who are offered a deal-type-aware "plan when you'll use this" action immediately post-checkout will reach a 30-day redemption rate **8pp higher absolute** than control (~55% → ~63%), with no degradation in CSAT and a refund-rate impact within the +2pp guardrail.

**Primary metric.** 30-day redemption rate, local-services first-purchase cohort.

**Guardrail.** Refund request rate within 30 days, same cohort. Stop condition: +2pp absolute.

**Secondary read-outs.** Median TTR (expect it to compress materially, not just shift), "How do I use this?" contact rate (expect a decrease as commitment is set at checkout), and the commitment-action take-rate (the leading indicator — see §5).

**What I'd need to believe.** That commitment-at-checkout actually shifts behavior, not just clicks (the implementation-intentions literature supports this; we should verify in this product). And that we have the merchant metadata (phone, booking URL, hours, sub-category) to make the per-deal-type CTA accurate at scale. If merchant data is incomplete in a critical sub-vertical, we ship E1 only in the sub-verticals where it works and explicitly defer the rest.

**Sample-size sanity.** Baseline ~55%, MDE 5pp absolute, 80% power, α=0.05 → ~1,500 per arm (~3,000 total). With weekly local first-purchase cohorts at Groupon's scale, this completes in under a week of exposure with margin for sub-category cuts.

---

### E2. AI-generated "what happens next" — deal-type and merchant-aware (post-purchase comms)

**Hypothesis.** Replacing the generic post-purchase confirmation with an AI-generated, deal-type-and-merchant-specific "what happens next" message (delivered in-app + email within one hour of purchase) lifts 7-day redemption rate **by 4pp absolute** vs. control, by collapsing the cognitive cost of identifying the right first action.

**Primary metric.** 7-day redemption rate, local cohort.

**Guardrail.** "How do I use this?" support-contact rate within 7 days. Stop condition: any increase. (If our AI content creates more confusion than it removes, we ship it back.)

**Pre-launch quality gate.** Eval set of 200 deals across local sub-categories; human review of accuracy on critical fields (merchant phone, booking URL, address, redemption type). Block launch on any hallucination of merchant-contact data. This is not a research demo; it's a production AI feature.

**What I'd need to believe.** That we can populate the per-deal-type next-step from merchant metadata that already exists in our marketplace. If we can't — if 30%+ of merchant records are missing critical fields — the AI generation degrades to generic and we ship something worse than the existing confirmation. Audit merchant data completeness *before* running E2.

**Sample-size sanity.** Baseline ~30% (7-day rate is lower than 30-day), 4pp MDE, 80% power, α=0.05 → ~2,200 per arm.

**Run order.** E1 first — the mechanism is higher-confidence and the result informs the prompt template for E2. E2 layered after E1 reads out, so we measure incremental lift over the commitment mechanic rather than against an untreated baseline.

## §5. Key metrics

**Outcome metrics.**
1. 30-day local-services redemption rate (the primary outcome of the redemption-clarity work).
2. 1st → 2nd purchase rate in 90 days, local cohort (the primary outcome of the retention work).
3. Refund request rate, local cohort (guardrail — we tolerate a defined increase; we won't tolerate an undefined one).

**Leading indicator.** **% of first-purchase buyers who take a commitment action within 48 hours of purchase**, where a commitment action is defined narrowly as: a confirmed merchant booking, a calendar entry created from our flow, or a user-set redemption-by date with reminder. This is the proxy for the H1 mechanism — did we successfully convert purchase into planned action — and it reads ~1–2 weeks before redemption-rate movement and ~6 weeks before repeat-rate movement.

**How this reads differently for local vs. goods.**

For **local**, the commitment-action rate is the *cause*. A rising rate predicts redemption ~1–2 weeks out and repeat ~6 weeks out. If commitment-action rises and redemption doesn't follow, we're collecting clicks, not behavior — the mechanic is failing to convert intent into action, and we should sharpen the Layer-2 hold before adding new Layer-1 surfaces.

For **goods**, the commitment-action rate is a *confound*, not an indicator. Goods has no booking, no date to pick — the package arrives when it arrives. The equivalent leading indicator for goods is **% of buyers who return to a Groupon surface within 7 days of delivery**. Goods buyers don't need to be activated *into* redemption (delivery does that); they need to be activated *back to the marketplace* in the moment they're feeling positive about the brand. If that 7-day-post-delivery return rate moves, repeat will follow. If it doesn't, "Buy Again" is firing in the wrong moment or in the wrong category for that cohort.

In one line: **for local we measure whether the user committed; for goods we measure whether the user returned.** The metric differs because the mechanism of redemption differs — and any team that uses one indicator for both deal types is reading goods through a local lens, which is exactly the mistake T3 warned us about.
